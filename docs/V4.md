# v4: puzzles in the street

v3.1 was honest cryptography inside a dull game. Played end to end, it was 46
steps of *walk somewhere, talk, open a terminal*, with about twenty thousand
words of story and instructions in between. Nothing happened between two
terminals, so the walking was dead time, and the systems added in 3.1 (the
Dragnet, allies, eCash, side quests) mostly added more reasons to walk. The
terminals themselves had turned into exercises: fill the slots of a formula,
follow the step list beside it, read a paragraph that already contains the
answer. No discovery, no opponent, nothing at stake, nothing to replay.

v4 keeps the city, the pixel art and the real cryptography, and changes what
you do in them. **The Wharf** is the first district rebuilt this way; it is
playable from the title screen as a preview (`THE WHARF · v4 PREVIEW`,
`CPL_WHARF=1`, `?wharf`) and keeps a save of its own. The v3.1 story is still
there, untouched.

## Rules for the rebuild

1. **One physical rule per district, learned by playing.** The Wharf's rule is
   a single sentence: *a card flips the lamps behind its holes.* Everything in
   the district (a gate, a guard's orders, a camera, the harbour mast) is a lamp
   board, and every puzzle is that rule applied to a new situation. The way
   The Witness teaches its panels: no tutorial text, a sequence of small
   rooms, each one changing one thing.
2. **Puzzles stand where they act.** A gate's lock is next to the gate; a
   patrol's orders hang in the alley he walks. Solving one changes the street
   you are standing in: the barrier drops, the guard turns west, the camera
   sleeps, the banners come down. Nobody hands out quests; the map (M) shows
   what is open, and one key takes you there.
3. **Real stakes, real time.** The street keeps moving while you work a board.
   The guard walks his round and raises your trace when he is close (the board
   says FOOTSTEPS, then SEEN); the camera sweeps; a lock that reads nonsense
   buzzes, one that reads ALARM calls it in. At 100 you are turned out of the
   district and every reader is emptied. The mast changes its pattern every
   26 seconds, so its puzzle has a clock.
4. **The cryptography is the playing field, not the text.** The lamps are a
   64-bit one-time pad (`cpl_crypto::lamps`), the seal is HMAC-SHA256
   truncated to 8 bits, the dives run on Lagrange interpolation over the reals
   and over GF(31) and on a genuine two-time pad. Nothing in the district is
   decrypted with a key. Every save has its own pads, digits and signs.
5. **Hints are opt-in and graded.** Each board has Grandpa's notebook (`?`):
   a nudge, then a push, then the answer. People in the street drop the
   facts a cryptanalyst would use ("the notice board shows the crest every
   day they have nothing to say").

## The Wharf, in order

| place | what you find out | the real thing behind it |
|---|---|---|
| the piers | a card is a pad; two cards combine; the right card turns noise into a picture | XOR, the one-time pad (Vernam 1917, Shannon 1949) |
| quay gate | a lock does what the picture says | a protocol acts on plaintext |
| training rig | flip a lamp on the board and the lock sees the same lamp flip | malleability: `C ⊕ Δ` decrypts to `M ⊕ Δ` |
| customs gate | the same, with no monitor: you know it says SHUT, so flip SHUT ⊕ OPEN | forging without the key |
| patrol orders | one reader, glass over the lamps, a guard on his round: cut the EAST ⊕ WEST card first | the same attack under pressure |
| camera control | WATCH ⊕ SLEEP, between sweeps | |
| fish market | a board that shows the crest on a quiet day gives up its pad, and the pad reads every other board on that console | known plaintext: `K = C ⊕ M` |
| ice house | two digits on one pad: stack the two rubbings and the pad cancels | the two-time pad, VENONA |
| market back gate | you cannot flip a difference into a picture you do not know, but you hold the pad now | |
| harbour master's shed | the tide board shows the anchor whenever the harbour is open | known plaintext again, for the mast's console |
| mole gate | a seal catches any change to the picture; record the drone's open signal and replay it | MACs stop forgery, not replay |
| the mast | a fresh rubbing erases whatever it says, the pad and your sigil draw what you want, before the pattern changes | all of it, against the clock |

Two optional dives (◆ on the map) sit between them. **INTERCEPTOR** (the
cabinet in the boathouse yard, a remake of the 2025 Lagrange prototype) is
Shamir's secret sharing as a shooting gallery: bend the curve through the
shares and fire a disc along it into the core at x = 0. Five shares and a
quadratic; a forged share that never lights with the others; two of three
keepers, where the free handle sweeps the whole column (the secret could be
anything); then the same game mod 31, where the curve becomes scattered
dots. **STATIC** (the radio shack) is crib dragging: two broadcasts on one
key, a bank of harbour words, and wherever a word is right the other message
reads as text too.

What it adds up to is in the end card: every lock on the Wharf used a perfect
cipher, and you never broke one. You broke how it was used.

## Ideas from the books, for the districts to come

Three books shaped this (Jarvis, *Crypto Wars*, 2020; Anderson, *Cypherpunk
Ethics*, 2022; Assange et al., *Cypherpunks*, 2012). What they offer a game is
less quotes than mechanics:

- **The surveillance labour constraint** (Jarvis, ch. 1). Mass surveillance
  used to be too expensive for a democracy to afford; digital collection
  removed the cost, and encryption is an attempt to put it back. A city-wide
  meter could make this literal: every tool you spread raises the price of
  watching everyone, and the Directorate falls back to watching *you*.
- **Three crypto wars as three districts.** War I (1966-81): the DES key
  length and the fight to publish (a brute-force dive against a 40/56-bit
  keyspace, a press that prints what may not be exported). War II (1991-2002):
  Clipper, the LEAF and its 16-bit checksum, PGP printed as a book (code is
  speech). War III (2013-): BULLRUN and a backdoored standard (a generator
  whose constants only the Directorate can explain), exceptional access.
- **The four horsemen** (May, 1994): every new law arrives with a frightening
  case attached. Districts could open with the Directorate's announcement and
  its pretext, and some choices should make the player's tools protect people
  they would rather not protect.
- **Conspiracy as a graph** (Assange, 2006, in Anderson ch. 4): a network of
  nails and twine whose links carry weight. Leaking does not cut a node; it
  makes every link costlier, until the network cannot think. A transparency
  district can be a graph puzzle where you thin links instead of capturing
  pieces.
- **Two ethics** (Anderson ch. 3): May's crypto anarchy (exit, markets, leave
  me alone) and Assange's crypto justice (courage, accountability, checks on
  power). Better than a points tally for the endings: the ending is *which*
  future the player built, and the "rats in the opera house" future, where
  only the technically skilled stay free, is the honest bad ending.
- **Convivial tools** (Illich, via Anderson ch. 2): a tool only counts if
  ordinary people can use it. Tools the player builds could have a usability
  cost that decides how far they spread.
- **Schneier's reflection** (quoted in Jarvis, ch. 7): mathematics is
  perfect, people and computers are not. The Wharf already ends on it; every
  district should have a lock that is mathematically sound and broken by
  how it is used.

| `crates/logic/src/story/wharf.rs` | the few things anyone says, being caught, the mast, walking out |
| `src/wharf.rs` | live lamp faces in the world, the guard, the drone |

The district is pure data plus a clock, so the tests play it without an
engine: `cargo test -p cpl-logic --test wharf` runs the autoplayer through
every board, dial and dive and out of the north gate on two seeds, and the
unit tests check that every site opens exactly when its gate does, that each
shortcut fails the way it should (a plain picture on an encrypted lock is
noise, a forged order breaks the seal, a stale rubbing of the mast is
worthless) and that glyphs peel apart unambiguously.
